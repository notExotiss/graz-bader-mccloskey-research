Abstract
Modularity-based community detection is dominated by the Louvain [ 4] and Leiden [ 20]
algorithms. Leiden fixed Louvain’s disconnected-community defect but left two long-standing
flaws of modularity itself untouched: the Fortunato–Barth´ elemy resolution limit [ 7] and the
Guimer` a spurious-community phenomenon on random graphs [9]. We introduce GRAZ (Greedy
Residual-Adjusted Z-test), a drop-in replacement for the move and aggregation rules of Lou-
vain/Leiden in which every candidate move is gated by the standardized Pearson residual of its
2 × 2 contingency table under the configuration-model null. Building on a 2010 sketch of Bader
and McCloskey [2], we derive a closed-form z-statistic for the single-node move and prove four
properties: (i) resolution-limit mitigation : on the ring of n cliques of size c, GRAZ with threshold
τ refuses the spurious merge whenever n ≤ (c2 − c + 2)/g(τ )2 where g(τ ) = (
√
τ 2 + 4 − τ )/2,
pushing the Fortunato–Barth´ elemy threshold back by an arbitrarily large factor; (ii)uniformly
bounded false-positive rate: the per-move false-merge probability under any configuration-model
null is bounded by 1 − Φ(τ ), independent of graph size or community-size heterogeneity; (iii)
convergence: under the conjunction rule z > τ∧ ∆Q >0, the modified Louvain/Leiden converges
in finite time with Q as Lyapunov function; (iv) regularized modularity: GRAZ is the greedy
optimizer of a heteroscedastic penalty on modularity in which the local regularization scale is set
by the variance of the null distribution at each move, producing a locally adaptive resolution
that is qualitatively distinct from the global resolution parameters of Reichardt–Bornholdt [ 17]
and CPM [ 19]. GRAZ requires four lines of code change in any existing Louvain or Leiden
implementation and replaces modularity’s only-remaining uncalibrated heuristic with a p-value
the practitioner can set.
1 Introduction
Community detection is the workhorse of network science across disciplines, from biological function
discovery to fraud detection to citation analysis. After two decades of method proliferation, the
practical landscape has consolidated: modularity [ 14] optimized by the Louvain algorithm [ 4], and
since 2019, by the Leiden algorithm [ 20], accounts for the overwhelming majority of community
detection performed in published research today. Leiden replaced Louvain in most pipelines because
it fixed a single named defect: Louvain’s communities could be internally disconnected, sometimes
catastrophically so [20].
Two flaws Leiden did not fix. Modularity has been known since 2007 to suffer from a resolution
limit [7]: it cannot resolve communities smaller than a graph-dependent scale, and on graphs with
size-heterogeneous communities it merges distinct small communities into a single super-community.

Independently, modularity has been known since 2004 to detect spurious community structure
in genuinely random graphs [ 9]: on an Erd˝ os–R´ enyi graph withm edges, modularity-maximizing

modularity for the Constant Potts Model (CPM) [ 19], which sidesteps the resolution limit at
the cost of giving up modularity’s principal advantage—its interpretability as a deviation from
a degree-preserving null [ 15]—or (b) bolting on post-hoc significance filters such as OSLOM [ 12],
which run as expensive refinement passes after Louvain/Leiden complete.
Contribution. We introduce GRAZ (Greedy Residual-Adjusted Z-test), a modification of
Louvain/Leiden that integrates statistical significance into the move-acceptance rule itself , requiring
no separate refinement pass and no change of objective. GRAZ extends a 2010 sketch of Bader and
McCloskey [2] for the agglomerative CNM algorithm [ 5] to the local-move framework that dominates
modern modularity optimization. The derivation reduces to a single closed-form z-statistic on the
2 × 2 contingency table of the proposed move under the configuration-model null; the move is
accepted only if its z-score exceeds a user-set threshold τ that is interpretable as the p-value gate.

regime of real networks—GRAZ implements a locally adaptive resolution that is qualitatively
distinct from any global resolution parameter.

2.3 Louvain and Leiden
The Louvain algorithm [ 4] alternates two phases. In the local-moving phase, each node i is in turn
considered for moving to a neighboring community B; the move is accepted if the resulting change
in modularity ∆ Q is positive, with ties broken by largest ∆ Q. In the aggregation phase, the current
partition is collapsed into a weighted multigraph in which each community becomes a super-node,
and the local-moving phase restarts. The process terminates when no move improves Q.
The Leiden algorithm [ 20] interposes a third phase, refinement, between local moving and
aggregation: each community is locally re-partitioned into well-connected sub-pieces, which become
the super-nodes of the aggregated graph. Refinement guarantees that no community is internally

disconnected, which can happen in Louvain. Leiden also exploits an explicit queue to revisit nodes
whose neighborhoods change, yielding a constant-factor speedup.
Both algorithms, however, retain the move-acceptance rule ∆ Q > 0, which is precisely the
zero-threshold Pearson test inherited from CNM. The two unresolved flaws—resolution limit and
spurious-community detection on random graphs—trace directly to this uncalibrated test.
2.4 Known flaws of modularity
Resolution limit. Fortunato and Barth´ elemy [7] proved that on the ring of n cliques of size c
connected by single edges, modularity is maximized by a partition that merges adjacent cliques
whenever n > c(c − 1) + 2. More generally, modularity-maximizing methods cannot resolve
communities smaller than roughly
p
m/2 edges, regardless of how clearly defined the communities
are in the underlying graph. Berry, Hendrickson, LaViolette and Phillips [ 3] subsequently showed
that edge-weighting schemes can mitigate the threshold, but no purely topological fix has been
published within the modularity-optimizing framework.
Spurious communities on random graphs. Guimer` a, Sales-Pardo and Amaral [9] showed that
on Erd˝ os–R´ enyi random graphs, modularity-maximizing methods return partitions withQ = Θ(1)
and approximately
p
m/2 communities, all of which are pure noise. Reichardt and Bornholdt [ 17]
confirmed the result analytically by viewing modularity as the ground state of a Potts Hamiltonian.
Instability . Even when Leiden finds a high-modularity partition, the partition depends sensitively
on node-visit order: on the same graph, two runs of Leiden with different random seeds can return
partitions with nontrivial normalized mutual information gap. This empirical instability has been
documented widely but lacks a clean theoretical fix within the modularity framework.

3.4 Choosing τ
The threshold τ admits two equivalent readings.
p-value reading. Under the configuration-model null each z statistic is asymptotically N (0, 1),
so τ = 2 gates moves at nominal p <0.05 and τ = 3 at p <0.0013. This is the practitioner-facing
knob.
Multiple-comparisons reading. If the practitioner wants the global false-discovery rate of
spurious communities to remain O(1) as m → ∞, the appropriate scaling is τm = √2 logm + c
for some slowly growing c, by an extreme-value argument analogous to Bonferroni correction; see
Theorem 2 and its discussion.
In practice we recommend τ = 2 as the default ( p <0.05 per move) and τ = 3 for high-stakes
applications. The empirical sensitivity to τ is the central question for the experimental design of
Section 6.

Remark 3 (Comparison to global resolution parameters) . Reichardt–Bornholdt [17] introduced a

This locally adaptive resolution is qualitatively distinct from any global γ. In particular, on a
graph with size-heterogeneous communities—e.g., the LFR benchmarks of Lancichinetti, Fortunato,
and Radicchi [11] with power-law community sizes, which is the standard testbed—global γ either
fragments large communities or merges small ones, while GRAZ’s local σ(i, B) adjusts to each
candidate move independently.
Remark 4 (Likelihood-ratio interpretation). Under the Gaussian approximation to the hypergeo-
metric null (valid for ki ≪ 2m via Berry–Esseen), the test ziB > τis precisely the size-(1 − Φ(τ ))

direction. GRAZ is therefore the greedy generalized-likelihood-ratio algorithm for community
detection against the configuration-model null, which establishes its asymptotic optimality among
all single-move tests against this null.
5 Related work
We position GRAZ against four families of prior methods.
Significance-based community detection. OSLOM [12] (Lancichinetti, Radicchi, Ramasco,
Fortunato) optimizes a fitness function based on the extreme-value statistics of individual-node
memberships, run as a refinement pass after an arbitrary clustering algorithm. ESSC (Wilson et
al. [21]) uses configuration-model significance for community extraction. GRAZ differs in three
respects. First, the significance test is integrated into the move-acceptance rule of Louvain/Leiden,
not appended as a refinement pass; this preserves the dominant pipeline rather than replacing it.
Second, the significance statistic is the standardized Pearson residual of a 2 × 2 contingency table,
which has closed-form variance and O(1) per-move cost; OSLOM and ESSC rely on extreme-value
or permutation-based estimates with higher per-move cost. Third, GRAZ inherits Louvain/Leiden’s
multilevel agglomeration structure, which neither OSLOM nor ESSC preserves.

CPM and resolution-limit-free objectives. Traag, Van Dooren, and Nesterov [ 19] introduced
the Constant Potts Model objective, which is resolution-limit-free. Leiden [ 20] can optimize CPM
in place of modularity, and this is the recommended path for users who care primarily about small-
community detection. However, CPM abandons modularity’s deviation-from-null interpretation
and replaces it with a density-threshold γ that has no a priori value; CPM also lacks the spurious-
community guarantee of Corollary 1 on random graphs (the constant-density null is not the same
as the configuration-model null). GRAZ retains modularity as the objective and obtains both
resolution-limit mitigation and spurious-community suppression as theorems.
Stochastic block model methods. Karrer and Newman [ 10] introduced the degree-corrected
SBM. Peixoto [16] developed MDL-based inference for nested SBMs, which avoids the resolution limit
and provides principled model selection. Decelle et al. [ 6] established the detectability threshold for
the symmetric SBM. SBM-based methods are statistically principled but require either EM/MCMC
inference (expensive) or block-merge agglomeration (similar in spirit to GRAZ but on a different
objective). GRAZ provides a modularity-native alternative with comparable statistical guarantees
and the operational simplicity of Louvain/Leiden.
The Bader–McCloskey precursor. The standardized-residual idea originates in a 2010 SIAM
workshop talk by Bader and McCloskey [ 2] that proposed the modification for the CNM agglom-
erative algorithm [ 5]. That sketch did not develop the single-node-move formulation, did not
prove convergence or resolution-limit theorems, did not connect to the regularized- modularity
interpretation, and predated the Leiden algorithm by nine years. GRAZ generalizes the sketch to
the modern local-move framework with full theory.
6 Experimental design and predicted outcomes
We specify six experiments that differentiate GRAZ from Louvain, Leiden, and CPM-Leiden. Two
of them yield exact closed-form predictions from the theorems and require no simulation noise
to interpret; the remaining four are empirical protocols with sharp qualitative predictions. All

Leiden [ 20], CPM-Leiden [ 19], and GRAZ at τ ∈ {1.5, 2, 2.5, 3}. Throughout, NMI denotes the
normalized mutual information of the recovered partition against ground truth.

6.6 Experiment 6: Real networks with metadata ground truth
Two small canonical instances and one large-scale benchmark.
Zachary’s karate club [22] (N = 34, m = 78, ground truth K = 2). Modularity-maximization
is famously over-segmenting on this network: Louvain and Leiden routinely return K = 4 at the
modularity optimum, while the social ground truth is K = 2 (the Mr. Hi and Officer factions).
The four-way split has internal connectedness weak enough that the standardized residuals on the
offending merges should fail the z >2 test, so GRAZ should return K = 2. Predicted F1 against
ground truth: Louvain/Leiden ≈ 0.7, GRAZ ≈ 1.0.
Adamic–Glance political blogs [ 23] (N ≈ 1,490, ground truth K = 2). The dataset has two
ground-truth communities (liberal vs. conservative). Louvain and Leiden typically return K ∈ [8, 14]
at modularity-optimum, fragmenting each faction. GRAZ at τ = 2 should return K ∈ [2, 4], with
the largest two communities aligning with the political labels. Predicted F1 against the two-block
ground truth: Louvain/Leiden ≈ 0.55, GRAZ ≈ 0.85.

7 Discussion and future work
7.1 A conjecture on SBM consistency
Conjecture 1 (Detectability). On the symmetric stochastic block model with k blocks of equal size
and intra/inter-block edge probabilities pin, pout, GRAZ with threshold τN = √2 logN achieves exact
recovery whenever the Decelle–Krzakala–Moore–Zdeborov´ a detectability threshold [6] is exceeded.
The basis for the conjecture is that the GRAZ test statistic is the standardized off-diagonal
Pearson residual, which is closely related to the dominant non-leading eigenvector of the modularity
matrix used in Newman’s spectral algorithm [ 15] and analyzed at the detectability threshold by
Nadakuditi and Newman [ 13]. A spectral proof in the style of [ 13], replacing the modularity matrix
with the standardized-residual matrix, appears to be the cleanest route. Proving Conjecture 1 would
establish GRAZ as the first modularity-native algorithm with provable SBM consistency at the
information-theoretic threshold; we leave it open.
7.2 Extensions
The GRAZ framework extends naturally in several directions worth pursuing.
Directed and weighted graphs. The 2 × 2 contingency-table derivation extends mechanically
to directed graphs (using the directed configuration model) and weighted graphs (using the weight-
preserving null). The variance formula eq. (5) acquires modest correction terms.
Overlapping communities. The OSLOM framework of Lancichinetti et al. [ 12] admits overlap
by allowing each node to have membership in multiple communities, each tested separately. A
GRAZ-overlap variant would gate each membership by its own z-test.
Streaming and dynamic graphs. Because the GRAZ test statistic is O(1) per move, the
algorithm extends to streaming and dynamic graphs in the same way Louvain does; the variance
update on a single edge insertion or deletion is constant-time.
Distributed implementation. The move-acceptance test is local: ziB depends only on i’s
neighbors and the degree sum of B. This locality is identical to Louvain’s and admits the same
distributed-memory parallelization strategies.
8 Conclusion
Louvain replaced CNM by exploiting local moves to obtain massive speed gains while preserving
modularity as the objective. Leiden replaced Louvain by adding a refinement phase that guarantees
connected communities. Both retained the move-acceptance rule ∆ Q >0, which is statistically
the zero-threshold Pearson test of an inter-community contingency table—an uncalibrated decision
inherited unchanged from the 1980s contingency-table literature. GRAZ replaces that uncalibrated
test with a z-test against the configuration-model null, gating every move by a single hyperparameter

τ with a direct p-value interpretation. The result is a drop-in replacement that mitigates the
resolution limit by an arbitrarily large factor, suppresses spurious communities on random graphs,
restricts the multiplicity of Louvain/Leiden fixed points, and admits an interpretation as locally
adaptive resolution. The cost is four lines of code; the gain is the first modularity-native algorithm
to address all three of modularity’s long-standing flaws simultaneously.
